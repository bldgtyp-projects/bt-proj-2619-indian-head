Carbon dioxide and the other pollution from burning fuel are the main drivers of warming in the Earth's atmosphere
and oceans. One widely used budget puts each person at roughly 2.3 tons of CO<sub>2</sub>e per year by 2030 for
all activities: about 1 ton for housing, 1 ton for transportation, and 0.3 tons for food. At the PHPP standard
occupancy of 3.5 people, the 1 ton per person housing figure puts this house's benchmark at about
**3.5 tons of CO<sub>2</sub>e per year**, shown as the reference line on the chart.

The chart compares the operating emissions of the five variants: the modeled electricity for heating, cooling,
hot water, appliances and fans, multiplied by the emission factor for the Connecticut grid. The factor is the
**EPA eGRID2023 rate for the New England (NEWE) subregion, 0.246 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per kWh**. See the
[EPA eGRID data explorer](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/data-explorer). The comparison covers building operation
only. Embodied carbon in the materials and construction, and household travel, are outside it.

Code minimum lands near 11.6 tons per year, the As-Drawn package near 8.3, the EnerPHit By Component
comparison near 6.2, and EnerPHit By Demand near 4.5. No variant reaches the 3.5 ton benchmark on operating emissions alone.
Two things would close the gap: rooftop solar, which is not yet in the model, and the New England grid, which
keeps getting cleaner each year. The second is already the main reason an all-electric house is the right call
here.

<div class="btwr-callout">
  CO<sub>2</sub>e emissions are calculated within the Passive House energy modeling software (PHPP), but they are
  not one of the certification metrics for Passive House projects. This comparison is for information only.
</div>
