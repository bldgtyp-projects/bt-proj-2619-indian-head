title: Building Airtightness

Airtightness does two jobs. In winter it keeps warm, moist indoor air out of the wall and roof cavities, where it would condense and damage the structure. In summer it keeps humid outdoor air out, which cuts cooling and dehumidification energy and keeps indoor humidity in range. It also has a direct relationship to heat loss: the tighter the house, the less heat escapes.

Code-minimum new construction in Connecticut is tested at 3.0 ACH50 (air changes per hour at a 50 Pascal test pressure). The renovation is modeled, and recommended, at **1.0 ACH50**, the EnerPHit airtightness limit. 

#### Why this house in particular

The architectural details already show an exterior sheathing membrane and an interior variable-permeance membrane. The work in the next phase is to resolve those into one continuous air-barrier line across the exterior walls, the roofs, the cellar boundary, the windows and doors, and the sunroom connection, with a detail for every transition. The places most likely to leak on this house are the roof intersections and dormers, the rim joists, the window bucks, the service entries, and the fireplaces.

The retained fireplaces deserve their own plan. Flue dampers, combustion-air paths and the chimney connection all sit on the air barrier, and the blower-door test should be run with them in their normal operating configuration rather than taped off, so the number reflects the house as it will be lived in.

#### How to hit the target

1. Mark the continuous air-barrier line on the sections and plans, and schedule every transition and penetration
   it has to survive.
2. Write **1.0 ACH50** into the construction documents as a contractual performance requirement.
3. Test as soon as the air barrier is complete and still accessible, before insulation and finishes cover it. A
   leak found then is a morning's repair; the same leak at completion is a demolition job.
4. Test again at completion for verification, and record the enclosure volume and test method with the result.
5. Include the kitchen exhaust, makeup-air dampers and fireplaces in the test plan, and budget for a second round
   of sealing.
