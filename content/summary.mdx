This report covers the design-phase energy modeling for the proposed home renovation in Riverside,
Connecticut. The project is a whole-house renovation and re-cladding, with replacement windows, exterior
insulation, a new energy recovery ventilation (ERV) system, and a sunroom addition. The performance model was built from
the **[Granoff Architects](https://granoffarchitects.com)** permit drawing set and the CAD files received on September 1, 2026. It carries 6,587 ft² of
treated floor area inside 18,335 ft² of thermal envelope.

We have tested five distinct _variants_ in the analysis here, from a code-minimum benchmark through the full PHI EnerPHit energy-demand target. The **As-Drawn variant** is the one we recommend in this case; the other four give it context. The findings below drive the recommendations in the pages that follow.

#### What the results show:

1. **The envelope work pays off:** The proposed renovation as drawn cuts purchased energy from about 47,100 kWh/yr at code minimum to about 33,800 kWh/yr, more than a quarter less, and cuts heating demand from about 58,200 to about 41,300 kWh/yr. Peak heating load drops from about 87 kBtu/h to about 60 kBtu/h (about 5 tons)—a size that a residential heat-pump system can handle easily.

2. **Airtightness and the ERV help:** The **As-Drawn variant** is modeled with an assumed 1.0 ACH50
   and a balanced ERV at about 73% effective heat recovery, and those two inputs cut the ventilation heat loss to
   less than half of the code-minimum case. Both require careful coordination during design and construction sequencing but do not have high materials costs, and both have to be written into the construction documents to be delivered.

3. **Two parts of the existing house stay as they are:** The fireplaces remain, and the crawlspace floor cannot be
   insulated. The floor is the larger energy cost of the two, about 5,200 kWh/yr of heating demand, and both are
   why we recommend the EnerPHit targets as a design guide rather than as a certificate. The EnerPHit By Component variant shows what the full component package would add on top of As-Drawn: heating demand falls to about 25,800 kWh/yr, and most of that step is the crawlspace floor and the junction detailing behind a lower
   thermal-bridge allowance.

4. **The LePage windows are a comfort decision, not an energy one:** At an installed U-0.20 they sit above the
   PHI winter-comfort threshold of U-0.165 for this climate, and the energy cost against a Passive House window
   is about 2,500 kWh/yr of heating demand. The comfort question matters at the sunroom, where the glass runs
   full height. See [Window Thermal Comfort](/windows/#window-thermal-comfort) for more details.

5. **The summer power-outage test indicates room for improvement:** In the **Phius REVIVE** week-long summer outage
   the above-grade floors spend 12 hours in the Heat Index Danger band. The winter test passes with a wide margin.
   Shading at the sunroom and the exposed upper windows could help improve this performance. See [Resilience](/resilience/) for more details.

#### What we recommend:

- Use the **As-Drawn** package as the basis of design for the envelope, windows and ventilation, and for pricing.
- Write **1.0 ACH50** into the construction documents as a contractual target, and test mid-construction while
  the air barrier is still accessible.
- Carry the LePage windows at an installed **U-0.20 Btu/hr-ft²-F** or better, and confirm that value with
  LePage's product data before the window order. Plan a heat source near the sunroom glazing, or ask LePage for
  a glazing package that reaches U-0.165 there.
- Plan the fresh-air system as a dedicated ERV with its own ducting, about 300 cfm at high speed, with a separate
  makeup-air system for the kitchen hood.
- Develop exterior shading at the sunroom and the exposed upper-story glazing before the window package is fixed.
