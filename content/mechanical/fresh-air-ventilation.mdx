An airtight, low-energy house in this climate needs dedicated mechanical ventilation with heat recovery to deliver
enough fresh air for good indoor air quality in every season. The recommended system is a balanced **energy
recovery ventilator (ERV)** with its own ductwork: supply air to the bedrooms and living spaces, transfer through
the halls, and extract from the bathrooms, laundry and the general kitchen area. The garage stays outside this
system.

#### Supply, transfer, extract

<figure class="btwr-section-hero"><img src="/assets/mechanical/cascade-ventilation.png" alt="Supply air passes through a bedroom and transfer zone before extraction from a bathroom." /></figure>

In this "cascade ventilation" arrangement, the same air ventilates the occupied rooms on its way to the extract rooms, so the house gets
full ventilation at a lower total airflow than supplying and extracting every room. That matters in winter: more
outdoor air than needed dries the house out and costs heating energy. Door undercuts or quiet transfer grilles
carry the air between rooms, sized for the intended flow and checked with the doors closed. The result is smaller
ducts, less duct-borne sound between rooms, and better mixing.

The ERV recovers heat from the outgoing air in winter and moderates humidity in both seasons. It handles the
day-to-day moisture exchange; cooling and dehumidification in a humid Connecticut summer remain the job of the
air-conditioning system.

#### Dedicated distribution

<div class="btwr-callout btwr-callout--critical">
  <strong>Coordination note</strong>
  <span>
    Install the fresh-air system so it is completely independent of the heating and cooling ductwork, and reserve
    its duct routes during the gut renovation. Ventilation then runs continuously whether or not there is a call
    for heating or cooling. Allocate space for both duct systems, with access and silencers where needed to keep
    fan noise out of the bedrooms.
  </span>
</div>

The As-Drawn package models a ventilation system at about **73% effective heat recovery**. The
effective figure counts the heat lost through the outdoor-air ducts, so it runs below the unit's
catalog rating; keeping those ducts short and well insulated is how the installed system reaches the modeled number. See [Ventilation System Balancing](/mechanical/#ventilation-system-balancing) for the equipment reference.
