The **Phius REVIVE** protocol uses a measurement called **"Heat Index"**, which combines air temperature and humidity into the temperature a person feels, to judge summer habitability. Every zone must satisfy both of the following criteria:

- **Zero hours** in the Danger band (above 103°F Heat Index).
- **Zero deadly days**, judged from the daily mean temperature and humidity.

<div class="btwr-callout btwr-callout--critical">
  <strong>Result: Danger band exceeded</strong>
  <span>Each above-grade level records 12 hours in the Danger band during the outage, with a peak Heat Index of about 109°F. No zone comes near Extreme Danger level (125°F). The cellar peaks at about 70°F Heat Index. The deadly-days check will be run with the final package.</span>
</div>

#### What the plots show

Indoor air on the above-grade floors peaks at about **88°F** while outdoor air reaches about **100°F**. Humidity is
what pushes the Heat Index into the Danger band: indoor relative humidity climbs to near **100%** in several zones
during the outage, and 88°F at that humidity feels like 109°F. The temperature alone would pass.

The four above-grade levels track each other closely, with the highest Heat Index around day 6 of the simulation.
The cellar behaves differently. Its air holds at about **68°F** through the whole week, but its humidity sits near
saturation as well, so it is cooler than the rest of the house rather than drier.

The three plots below show Heat Index, air temperature and relative humidity. Hover for hourly values and use the
legend to isolate zones. Outdoor conditions are plotted alongside the indoor ones.

#### What would change the result

1. **Exterior shading at the sunroom and the exposed upper glazing.** Overhangs, operable exterior shades or
   planting stop the sun before it crosses the glass, and they are the strongest passive measure available on
   this house. Coordinate them with the [summer radiation study](/windows/#summer-radiation), and choose devices
   that can be worked by hand during an outage.
2. **Specify the interior shades.** Choose a light-colored, tightly woven blind which will block several times as much heat as a dark open-weave fabric.
3. **Set the SHGC at the exposed openings.** All five variants carry a 0.40 SHGC. A lower value at the most
   exposed windows cuts the gain directly, at some cost in winter gain at those openings. _Note: LePage window product information received Sept 10, 2026 (after this study was complete) indicates an SHGC of ~0.20, which would help reduce this overheating risk significantly._
4. **Plan backup cooling and dehumidification.** Much of the load is humidity and the occupants' own heat, which
   no envelope measure removes. A modest battery or generator serving one heat pump and a dehumidifier in a
   chosen refuge room is the measure that closes the gap with certainty. Choose the rooms and the loads first,
   then size the equipment.
5. **Window opening helps only when outdoor air is cooler and drier than indoors.** During this heat wave those
   hours are few, so larger openings would change little.
